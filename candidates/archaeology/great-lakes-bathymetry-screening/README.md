# One wreck-shaped body in open Great Lakes lidar bathymetry; the detector is not calibrated

Status: **candidate, weak.** The position is withheld. Produced with AI assistance; see the repository
README. Nothing has been reported to anyone.

## What was screened

NOAA's public topographic-bathymetric lidar elevation models for three shallow areas of the upper Great
Lakes: eastern Lake Superior (23 tiles), the Thunder Bay area of Lake Huron (26 tiles) and the islands
of northern Lake Michigan (3356 tiles). A detector looked for elongated bodies standing above the lake
bed: a smoothed bed is subtracted, and connected residuals are kept if their length, width, height and
shape could fit a hull. It produced 895, about 1800 and 4624 detections in the three areas. They were
ranked, looked at on contact sheets, and compared with the wrecks and obstructions on NOAA's electronic
charts (9337 charted features in the upper lakes).

## What was found

One body in eastern Lake Superior, in Michigan waters: about 55 m long and 8 to 12 m wide, standing 2.6 m
above the bed in about 10.5 m of water. Hulls of nineteenth-century lake freighters have such
proportions. So does a short glacial or bedrock ridge, and the lidar alone cannot tell them apart. The
first pass graded it B minus.

Nothing was kept from the other two areas.

## Calibration, stated plainly

The detector has **not** been shown to find a known wreck. Of 5519 detections in the Lake Superior and
Lake Michigan areas, six lie within 100 m of a charted wreck or obstruction. In the Thunder Bay tiles
the nearest wreck in the list used is 823 m from any detection. The first pass did not establish how
many charted wrecks lie inside the surveyed water at all, so no recovery rate can be given. Until the
detector is run over a few wrecks whose positions are published, this candidate is a shape, not a find.

## What the second pass did

It recomputed the counts and distances in the previous section from the first pass's detection tables.
It did not download the elevation tiles again or measure the body again; the size, height and depth are
the first pass's.

## Not checked

State wreck inventories, the lists of the regional shipwreck societies and museums, sanctuary
inventories, and sonar surveys. This water has been searched by wreck hunters for decades; a body of
this size in 10 m of water is more likely known or natural than new.

## What would settle it

A side-scan sonar pass or a dive. The proper recipient of the position is the state maritime
archaeologist.

## Data and credits

data (ENC Direct).
